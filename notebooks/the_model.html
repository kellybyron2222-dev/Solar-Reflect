Orbital sunlight on Earth

What the math is saying, in order. Open this file in a browser so the equations typeset. Each equation also has a one-line plain version.

What we are asking

Imagine a flat mirror in low Earth orbit that reflects sunlight onto the ground. For a given collecting area (how big the mirror is) and altitude (how high it flies), three numbers fall out:

  1. How bright is the patch on the ground? (watts per square metre)
  2. How big is that patch? (kilometres across)
  3. How long does an overhead pass last? (minutes)

Then: which physical fact binds first — not enough collected energy (the Sun is a disk, so the patch cannot be squeezed arbitrarily small and bright), or the satellite races past (the orbital shutter)?

Uses such as moonlight, street lighting, or solar panels are compared after those three numbers exist. Cost, companies, and 3D pictures are not this model.

The geometry is dusk/dawn, looking straight down: the satellite is over the terminator, the ground site is at the satellite’s footpoint, the Sun is 90° from nadir, so the mirror is folded at 45°. That is not midnight over a dark city.

Names (say them out loud)

NameSymbolMeaning
Altitude$h$Height above Earth. Grid: 400, 625, 1000, 2000 km.
Collecting area$A$Mirror area in m². Example markers: 18 m square, 55 m square, 1 km square.
Optical factor$\eta$Mirror reflectance × atmosphere. Ideal = 1. Estimated clear sky = 0.9 × 0.75 = 0.675.
Solar constant$E_0$Sunlight in space: 1361 W/m² (AM0, above the atmosphere).

Elevation$\varepsilon$How high the satellite sits in the sky from the ground site. Brightness uses 90° (straight up). Timing uses a 30° cut.
Sun’s angular size$\alpha$Full width of the solar disk as seen from Earth: 0.0093 radians (~0.53°).
Slant range$d$Mirror-to-ground distance. Looking straight down, $d = h$.
Patch diameter$D$Width of the Sun’s image on the ground.
Patch area$A_{\mathrm{image}}$Area of that disk.
Peak irradiance$I$Brightness on the filled patch, W/m².
Useful dwell$T_{\mathrm{useful}}$Time the satellite is above 30° elevation on an overhead pass.

1. How big is the patch?

The Sun is not a point. It is a disk. A mirror — flat or focusing — cannot make a ground spot smaller than the Sun’s angular size times the distance to the ground.

$$d = h \qquad D = d\,\alpha \qquad A_{\mathrm{image}} = \pi \left(\frac{D}{2}\right)^2$$

d = h

D = d * alpha

A_image = pi * (D / 2)^2

Focusing does not shrink D. Compute D in metres before squaring. Alpha is the full solar diameter, not the radius.

At 625 km: D = 5.8 km. The 55 m mirror is a speck inside that patch. Brightness is collected watts spread over that whole disk, not a searchlight beam.

2. How bright is the patch?

Power collected by the mirror, then spread over the solar image on the ground. Not inverse-square from a lamp.

$$I = \frac{E_0\,\eta\,A\cos\gamma\sin\varepsilon}{\pi (d\alpha/2)^2}$$

I = E0 * eta * A * cos(gamma) * sin(epsilon) / A_image

For the snapshot: epsilon = 90°, so sin(epsilon) = 1. gamma = 45°, so cos(gamma) = 0.707. Pass eta as one number (1 or 0.675). Do not multiply eta, rho, and tau together.

In words: sunlight in space × efficiency × mirror area × cosine of the fold, divided by the area of the Sun’s image on the ground.

I scales with A (bigger mirror, more watts). I scales as 1/h² because the patch area grows with h² — that is the solar disk, not a lightbulb. Clouds are not in this peak-I formula (clear-sky envelope only).

3. How long does a pass last?

The satellite is in a circular orbit. Period uses the orbit radius (Earth radius plus altitude), not altitude alone.

$$a = R_E + h \qquad T = 2\pi\sqrt{a^3/\mu}$$

a = R_earth + h

T_period = 2 * pi * sqrt(a^3 / mu)

mu is Earth’s gravitational parameter. Using sqrt(h^3 / mu) would be wrong by a huge factor.

From the ground, the satellite is “useful” while its elevation is above 30°. The Earth-central angle from overhead to that contour is:

$$\theta(\varepsilon) = \arccos\!\big((R_E/a)\cos\varepsilon\big) - \varepsilon$$ $$T_{\mathrm{useful}} = T \cdot \theta(\varepsilon_{\min}) / \pi$$

theta(eps) = arccos((R_earth / a) * cos(eps)) - eps   (angles in radians)

T_useful = T_period * theta(30°) / pi

That is the full overhead (zenith-track) window. Off-track passes are shorter. Eclipse is not subtracted. T_useful is in seconds in the code; divide by 60 for minutes.

An energy-per-pass envelope is I times T_useful. That is an upper bound: brightness is not constant through the pass, and night-side shadow is not cut out.

Worked example (checked)

Altitude 625 km, 55 m square mirror (A = 3025 m²), looking straight down at dusk/dawn. These numbers match the calculator and the tests to better than 1%.

QuantityIdeal (η = 1)Estimated (η = 0.675)
Patch diameter D5.81 km
Patch area2.65 × 10⁷ m²
Peak irradiance I0.110 W/m²0.074 W/m²

For scale: full sunlight on the ground is about 1000 W/m². Full moonlight is on the order of 0.003 W/m² (estimated). A 55 m mirror at 625 km is far closer to moonlight-class than to daylight.

That is a calibration point, not the study. The study still has to sweep area and altitude, then sit the numbers against lighting and PV thresholds.
