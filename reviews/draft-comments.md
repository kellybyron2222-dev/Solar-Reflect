#### The flashlight picture

>
> The picture in full: a giant flashlight in space. A reflective sheet unfolds above Earth, points at a city, and shines a bright spot wherever we want light. It sounds plausible because mirrors do redirect sunlight, and orbit is above the night. Dawn and dusk put the Sun on the horizon while cities below are already going dark. Tilt a sheet, fold that light down, paint the streets. A 55-metre sheet of reflector — a foil✓, from here on — is the size of a building. A building-sized lamp, hung 625 km up, ought to do something dramatic. Hold that picture. The rest of this note has to earn the right to replace it.

The intuitive picture is simple: a giant flashlight in space.

Imagine a 55-square-metre mirror unfolding in orbit above Earth. The mirror points at a city and redirects sunlight to create a bright spot wherever light is wanted. At first glance, the idea seems plausible. Mirrors can reflect sunlight, and an object in orbit may still be illuminated by the Sun while the ground below is in darkness.

That mental model is powerful because it feels familiar. We already know that mirrors can throw bright patches of light across a room, across a field, or even across long distances. Why not scale the same idea up and do it from space?

The question is how closely the underlying physics matches the simple picture that first comes to mind. To find out, we have to treat the concept as a real optical system governed by geometry, diffraction, orbital mechanics, and energy conservation. The flashlight analogy is a useful starting point, but it captures only part of the story.
